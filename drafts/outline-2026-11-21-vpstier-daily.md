## 1. Rationale + Hot-Source

**Why this topic, and why today.** On **18 November 2026** DediRock — a whitelisted provider with a live `/vps/dedirock/` profile and a published review on this site — launched a product line that does not belong to the 2026 VPS market this site has been writing about. The **High-RAM VPS** line sells **8 GB of RAM for $24.99/year**, then 16 GB for $59.99/yr, then 32 GB for $89.99/yr. In a year when this site published **"VPS Price Hikes 2026: The RAMageddon"** — an article whose own thesis is that the 2026 memory crunch has pushed per-GB RAM prices sharply upward — a budget host putting **8 GB on spinning disk for less than the price of a single 1 GB SSD box** is a genuine data point against the trend this site has documented.

**The story that is actually new (and the one no outlet wrote):** the launch is interesting not because it is cheap, but because of **what it trades away**. Every tier pairs the unusually large memory allocation with **1 vCPU (2 vCPU only on the 32 GB tier), spinning HDD rather than NVMe, and the vendor's own explicit warning that the line is "not intended for heavy disk I/O or sustained CPU-intensive workloads."** That is a **deliberate architecture trade**, not a discount — memory capacity bought with I/O latency and CPU headroom. For a VPS buyer, the useful question is not "is 8 GB for $25 good?" but "**which workloads actually want 8 GB of RAM and do not care about disk speed?**" — and the honest answer, from the vendor's own sizing guidance, is a specific and fairly narrow set.

## 4. FAQ (5 questions)

1. **Is 8 GB of RAM for $24.99 a year really the cheapest 8 GB I can get?** It is the cheapest **8 GB on spinning disk** we can verify, and the gap is large: the RackNerd specials ladder read on 21 November 2026 puts its 8 GB NVMe/SSD plan at **$94.98/yr** — roughly **3.8× more per GB**. Second, this is **one provider's one product line in one US city** — it is not a market-wide price collapse, and this site's own coverage of the 2026 memory crunch still stands. And the price comes with **1 vCore and a spinning disk**.
2. **Does the $24.99 price stay the same when it renews?** The vendor said so publicly. In the comment thread under the launch post, on **20 November 2026**, a reader asked whether the prices were "locked in or ... only for the first year" and the vendor's account replied: **"They are locked in! Renews at the same price!"** Read that alongside the vendor's own Terms of Service, which say recurring billing happens only where the customer has expressly authorised it, and the Refund Policy, which says all products are non-refundable once payment has been received. Our honest reading: **that is a support-reply price lock, not a contractual price lock**, and the operative document is the TOS. Check whether auto-renew is enabled on your account, and treat the renewal as the real price when you are sizing a multi-year budget.
3. **Is a 1 vCore box with 8 GB of RAM a good idea?** It depends entirely on which of the two your workload wants. **One core is the constraint long before 8 GB is the constraint** for most software — a database, a web server and a background job on a single core will show the CPU as the bottleneck even with the memory to spare. The cases where 8 GB on one core genuinely works are workloads that are *memory-bound rather than compute-bound*: a database with a hot working set, a cache, a build cache, or several small services that are mostly idle. The vendor's own sizing table, published 20 November 2026, puts "busy application, multiple services, or heavier database use" at **4+ vCPUs and 8 GB+ RAM** — so on the vendor's own numbers, this 8 GB tier sits a tier below what its 8 GB band calls for. That is worth knowing before you buy.
4. **Is the disk really spinning, and does that matter?** Yes on both counts, per the published spec table: the line ships **20 GB (Core), 30 GB (Plus) and 50 GB (Power) of HDD storage**, and the vendor's own positioning says the line is "**not intended for heavy disk I/O**." It matters enormously for anything that reads or writes a lot — a database with a large dataset on disk, a media server, a build that writes artifacts, an OS with a slow disk will feel it on every operation. For a workload whose working set lives in RAM and whose disk traffic is small, 20 GB of spinning disk is not the bottleneck and 8 GB of RAM is a bargain. The single most important thing to confirm at checkout is that the storage media is what you think it is, because the same vendor advertises NVMe prominently on its other lines.
5. **Which regions does it run in, and is that a problem for me?** The High-RAM line is **Buffalo, New York only** — the vendor's BUF1 facility in Western New York, with a looking glass at `199.188.100.133`. The vendor's second location is LA1 in Downtown Los Angeles, but the High-RAM line is not offered there. For North American users that is fine; for Europe or Asia-Pacific it means a transatlantic or transpacific round trip for every request, and the latency-sensitive workloads this line is otherwise good for are exactly the ones that suffer. The vendor does describe the "upstate" location as offering "superior connectivity and latency to Europe," but the way to check that claim is to run your own tests against the published looking-glass address from your own network before you commit to a year.
