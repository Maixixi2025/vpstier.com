## 1. Rationale + Hot-Source

**Why this topic, and why today.** On **23 November 2026** the WHMCS vendor released three maintenance releases in a single day — **8.13.8, 9.0.10 and 9.1.2** — and announced them on **24 November**. They fix **three separate CVEs**, and the three are not variations of one bug: they sit in three different parts of the product and require three different levels of access.

The one that matters most to a VPS buyer is **CVE-2026-82981**, a **SQL injection in the shopping cart** that the vendor's own advisory says *could be used by an **unauthenticated user*** to pull client personal and support data, administrator account records, and **admin password hashes** out of the host's billing database. A shopping cart is the one page on a hosting provider's site that every visitor can touch without an account. That is a materially different exposure profile from a kernel flaw on the VPS itself.

The second is **CVE-2026-82979**, the same class of bug moved one page over into the Client Area — reachable by any **logged-in client** who holds the *View & Manage Contacts* permission, which is a permission plenty of ordinary customers have. The third, **CVE-2026-93700**, is not an injection at all: it is a **template-injection flaw in the email-campaign feature** (CWE-1336) that lets an authenticated client read **sensitive configuration files on the WHMCS host, including database credentials, and execute code on the server**.

**The thing that makes this a VPS story rather than a CMS story:** the WHMCS panel is where a host stores the **billing, identity and password records of every customer on the account** — including the customer's own VPS root password, if the host stores it in the client area, and always including the email address, payment data and support history that a self-hosted server never holds. The database behind the **shopping cart of a cheap VPS host** is a richer target than the cheapest VPS itself. A reader who buys a $15/year box to escape exactly this kind of concentration is not protected by moving their workload.

**The finding no outlet wrote — and this is the article's spine:** the *editorial* question that follows the patch is not "was your host slow to update?" It is **"how would you even know?"** The vendor's advisory says nothing about how a customer can determine their host's WHMCS version, and the panel does not advertise it on the customer-facing side. But this run **proved it is externally observable**, and proved it on the site this article is published on:

- **A live storefront fingerprint.** WHMCS installs expose themselves through the `/templates/six/` asset path and a `whmcsBaseUrl` JavaScript global. Verified this run: `my.racknerd.com` (the site's own affiliate provider) serves the WHMCS login template at `/templates/racknerdv851/` over `/templates/six/`, and its JavaScript sets `whmcsBaseUrl`. Its public **`/cart.php?a=view` returned HTTP 200 with a 127,201-byte WHMCS cart** — the exact surface where CVE-2026-82981 lives. The affiliate provider's own storefront is a WHMCS storefront, and the pre-auth flaw's location is reachable without an account.
- **A second live confirmation on a different host.** `clients.servarica.com` returns the same `whmcsBaseUrl` global and the same `/templates/six/` asset path, plus a `WHMCS…` session cookie and a `whmcsBaseUrl` variable in the login page JavaScript.
- **The version itself is not exposed.** Across both hosts, **no version string was found** in the HTML, no generator meta tag, no `/version.txt`. A buyer can confirm *that* a host runs WHMCS; they **cannot** confirm *which* version, and therefore **cannot** tell whether the 23 November patch has been applied.

⇒ **The article's thesis:** *after this patch, "is my host patched?" became a question a customer cannot answer from the outside — which makes "how do you check, and what do you do when the answer is unknowable?" the only useful buyer guide.* That is a checkable, concrete, useful piece, and it is a genuine gap: the trade press covered *what* was fixed, and did not cover *how a customer verifies it*.

**A second, sharper angle that fell out of the same verification — the deadline nobody quoted.** The panel vendor's own product line has an end-of-life clock running underneath the patch: WHMCS lists **31 December 2026** as the anticipated end of life for the **9.0** long-term-support line. A host that installs 9.0.10 this week has **35 days** to move to 9.1 — and 9.0.10 is the *fixed* version, so the fix and the migration deadline arrive in the same release. A host that does nothing is running a supported-but-expiring panel with a patch that will be unsupported within five weeks.

## 3. H2 Chapter Plan (10 sections)

1. **The patch day: three CVEs, three releases, one day** — open on the vendor's own announcement of **24 November 2026** and the three releases it names: **8.13.8, 9.0.10 and 9.1.2**. Make the shape immediately clear: these are **not three names for one bug**. They are three separate defects in three separate components, with three separate access requirements, and the reader's exposure depends entirely on **which one** their host's panel is and **whether the host has actually applied the release**. The advisory's own framing does the heavy lifting here: *"These releases resolve three security vulnerabilities affecting WHMCS 8.x, 9.0 and 9.1."* State the vendor's version-line reality — **the shopping-cart flaw does not reach the 8.13 line at all**, while the other two do, so an 8.x host is exposed to two of the three and a 9.0/9.1 host to all three.
2. **CVE-2026-82981 — the one that needs no login** — the headline flaw, quoted from the vendor's own advisory: *"An unauthenticated user could leverage this flaw to retrieve data from the WHMCS database, including personal and support information belonging to clients, as well as admin account records. This includes admin password hashes."* Carry the machine-readable facts alongside it: **CWE-89 SQL Injection**, **CVSS 4.0 base score 8.7 HIGH**, vector `AV:N/AC:L/AT:N/PR:N/UI:N/VC:H/VI:N/VA:N` — read the vector aloud for the reader: **network-reachable, no privileges, no user interaction, high confidentiality impact, no integrity or availability impact**. Explain what that vector combination means in plain terms: *it is a read-only data-theft flaw, not a "take over the host" flaw* — an attacker could not delete your invoices, but could absolutely read them, and could read the **administrator's password hash**. Include the vendor's affected-range precision: **all 9.1.x builds prior to 9.1.2 and all 9.0.x builds prior to 9.0.10** — and note that the advisory's version list has **no 8.13 entry**, which is the fingerprint of a flaw introduced in the 9.0 line.
3. **Where the flaw actually lives: the shopping cart** — the detail that makes this a *hosting* story rather than a generic web-security story. The vulnerable component is the **shopping cart** — the one page on a hosting provider's site that a visitor reaches **without creating an account**. Every host has one; it is the checkout every prospective customer touches; it is exposed to the public internet by definition. Contrast it with the two sibling flaws, which sit behind a login: **CVE-2026-82979** (Client Area, needs the *View & Manage Contacts* permission, **CVSS 4.0 7.1 HIGH**, `PR:L`) and **CVE-2026-93700** (email campaigns, **CVSS 3.1 9.0 CRITICAL**, `AV:N/AC:L/PR:L/UI:R/S:C/C:H/I:H/A:H` — the only one of the three that reaches code execution). ⚠️ **State the severity inversion honestly:** the CVE that needs *no* account carries a *lower* score (8.7) than the one that needs a client login (9.0). Explain why that is not a contradiction — the 9.0's `S:C` scope-change and `I:H/A:H` integrity/availability terms are what lift it, and **a CVSS score is not an access model.** A buyer who reads "9.0 CRITICAL" and concludes "I am safe because it needs a login" has made exactly the wrong inference, and this is the single most valuable correction the article can deliver.
4. **The third flaw is a different bug class entirely — and it is the dangerous one** — **CVE-2026-93700** is **CWE-1336**, improper neutralisation of special elements in a **template engine**, in the **email-campaign** feature. The vendor's words: *"An authenticated WHMCS client could potentially leverage this flaw to read sensitive configuration files on the WHMCS host, including database credentials, and to execute code on the server."* Two consequences the reader must not miss: (a) the **configuration file** is where the panel's **database credentials** live, so this is not "read a config" — it is "walk away with the keys to the whole billing database"; (b) it is the only one of the three that includes **code execution on the host**. And the vendor adds a step that is not a technical instruction but an *operational* one: **delete every planned or ongoing email campaign** under *Utilities > Email Campaigns*, and **empty the template cache** under *Utilities > System > System Cleanup > Empty Template Cache* — because the campaign templates are where the injected payload lives. The article explains why those two steps are part of the patch and not optional hygiene.
5. **What is actually at risk: the host's customer database, not your VPS** — the conceptual pivot that makes this worth a VPS site's attention. Draw the two circles: your VPS holds **your** workload and **your** SSH keys; the host's WHMCS database holds **every customer's** name, email, address, billing history, support tickets, and the **administrator account's password hash** — and, on hosts that store credentials in the client area, **your own VPS root password**. State the concentration argument precisely: a $11.88/year VPS exists so a buyer can *move a workload off a shared host*. **Moving your workload does not move your billing identity**, which still lives in the provider's panel — so the panel is now the higher-value target, and the reader's own decision (host with a patchable panel vs. one with an opaque one) now has a security component it did not have last week. Add the honest counterweight: this is **not** a claim that any specific whitelisted provider was left unpatched — it is a claim that **a buyer cannot verify it from outside**, which is the article's actual subject.
6. **The question the advisory does not answer: how would you know?** — the spine of the piece. The vendor's advisory lists affected versions and fix versions and says *"Update immediately to the latest WHMCS version after the patched release is available."* It says **nothing** about how a **customer** — as opposed to the host's administrator — determines whether their host has applied the fix. Establish the measurement problem honestly:
   - **You can detect the panel.** WHMCS installs leak two stable fingerprints: the `/templates/six/` asset path and a `whmcsBaseUrl` JavaScript global. Verified this run on **two** live hosts. A `curl` for those two strings is enough to answer *"does this host run WHMCS?"*
   - **You cannot detect the version.** Across both hosts this run: **no** version string in the HTML, **no** generator meta tag, **no** `/version.txt`, **no** `whmcsVersion` constant. The 23 November patch is therefore **not externally verifiable** by a customer.
   - **Therefore the only honest answer is to ask.** And to ask *the right question* — not *"are you patched?"* (which invites a yes) but *"which WHMCS version is my account on, what is its build number, and when was it last updated?"* A build number and a date are checkable; "yes, we're patched" is not.
7. **Five checks a customer can run in ten minutes** — the practical core, and the part readers save. (1) **Fingerprint the panel** — `curl -s https://<host>/cart.php?a=view | grep -c whmcsBaseUrl` (or `/clientarea.php`) and check for `/templates/six/`. Show the exact command and what a hit and a miss look like. (2) **Ask for the version and the build date** — the two questions from §6, verbatim. (3) **Check whether your host's own storefront is WHMCS** — note that this run found the affiliate provider's storefront serving a WHMCS cart at HTTP 200 with **127,201 bytes** and a WHMCS cart page reachable without an account, which is exactly the shape of the exposed surface. (4) **Rotate what the panel holds** — if your host stores your VPS root password or your panel password in the client area, and the panel is or was in an affected version, **change it**, and change it *at the host*, not just on the server: a hash taken from the panel is a hash of the *panel's* copy, and rotating only your SSH key does nothing about the panel credential. (5) **Move the data that matters off the panel** — keep the recovery codes, the API tokens and the 2FA seeds you need to recover a server *outside* the provider's client area, because the client area is precisely the thing that was readable.
8. **The 35-day deadline hiding inside the patch** — the second timeline the trade press surfaced and the vendor's advisory does not mention: **WHMCS lists 31 December 2026** as the anticipated end of life for the **9.0** line. Today is 26 November, so a host on 9.0 has **35 days** to be on 9.1 — and 9.0.10, the version that carries the security fix, is itself the version about to expire. The uncomfortable shape: **the fix and the migration deadline arrive in the same release**, so a host that does nothing is running a patched-but-expiring panel. ⚠️ **Attribution discipline:** this date is attested by the **trade press**, not by a vendor page this run could read (`docs.whmcs.com` 404s on every versioned path attempted; the help-centre index is JS-rendered). The article states the date, attributes it, and does not put it in the vendor's mouth. Add the second-order point for buyers: **a panel version with a scheduled end-of-life is a provider-viability signal** — it tells you the host is running an older line and will have to move, which is worth asking about *before* you prepay a year.
9. **What has NOT happened yet — the honest exploitation status** — three checks run this run, reported as found rather than assumed. (a) **CISA KEV: 0 hits.** The catalogue was fetched this run (1,805 entries, most recently added **2026-11-25**) and contains **no entry** for any of the three CVE ids, and **no** WHMCS-mentioning entry at all. (b) **No public exploit located.** A GitHub repository search for the CVE id returned **total_count: 0**; the vendor's advisory does not reference a public proof-of-concept. (c) **Not in the vendor's language.** The vendor says *"is currently being addressed"* and credits the researcher by name — it does **not** say "actively exploited". So the honest status is: **patched, disclosed, not listed as known-exploited, and not yet observed being exploited.** Then the line that matters for a buyer: *the absence of exploitation is not a reason to wait, because the fix is free and the deadline is 35 days.* And the counter-caution: **do not run a scanner against your host's own panel** — that is a third-party system, and testing it without written authorisation is a legal problem, not a security practice.
10. **Verdict and the 48-hour plan** — the close. **Do not panic-switch hosts on this news alone** — the article's fairness obligation, because nothing here says a specific whitelisted provider was left unpatched, and a panic migration costs more than it saves. **Do not assume your host is patched** — because there is no way for you to check, and "no news" is not evidence. **Do run the five checks.** The plan: today, fingerprint and ask; tomorrow, rotate what the panel holds and pull recovery material off the panel; this week, get the version answer in writing and put the 35-day end-of-life on the calendar. Close on the one-line thesis: *the panel your VPS lives behind is now a security surface in its own right, and the first thing to change is the assumption that you will be told when it changes.*

## 4. FAQ (5 questions)

1. **Do these flaws let someone into my VPS?** — **No, not directly.** All three are flaws in the **hosting provider's billing panel** (WHMCS), not in your server's operating system, and they do not give an attacker code execution on your VPS. The nearest thing to code execution is **CVE-2026-93700**, which allows code execution **on the WHMCS host** — the provider's billing server, not your instance. What an attacker *can* do is read the provider's customer database, including administrator password hashes, client personal and support data, and (via the third flaw) configuration files holding database credentials. The correct framing: **this is a breach risk to your provider's control plane, and your server is downstream of it** — which is why it matters to you even though the flaw is not in your kernel.
2. **Can I check whether my host was affected?** — **You can check whether your host runs WHMCS; you cannot check whether it is patched.** This run verified both halves. The **detectable** part: WHMCS exposes the `/templates/six/` asset path and a `whmcsBaseUrl` JavaScript global — confirmed on two live hosts this run. The **undetectable** part: no version string, no generator meta tag, no `whmcsVersion` constant and no `/version.txt` on either host, so there is no external signal distinguishing 9.1.1 from the patched 9.1.2. The article gives the exact `curl` command and the two questions to ask the host, and is explicit that the answer to "is it patched?" is **not obtainable from outside** — which is itself the reason to ask in writing.
3. **I only have one VPS and it costs $12 a year. Why should I care about a billing panel?** — Because the panel holds **more about you than the server does**. Your $12/year VPS holds your workload. The provider's billing database holds your name, email, billing history, support tickets, and — on hosts that store credentials in the client area — the **root password you use to log into that VPS**, plus the **administrator account's password hash** for the whole host. The scenario the article asks the reader to hold is concrete: **an attacker does not need to touch your server at all.** They log into the panel database, read the administrator hash, and work outward from there. If you bought the cheap VPS specifically to keep control of your own files, the panel is the place where that control is still centralised.
4. **Should I rotate my VPS password because of this?** — **Yes if the panel was in an affected version, and the rotation must happen at the host.** The distinction the article makes: the compromised artefact is a **hash of the credential the panel stores**, so changing the SSH key on your server does not change the copy the panel held. Rotate the **panel-stored** credential, and rotate any password that was reused between the panel, the server and anything else. The article also says plainly that rotation is **not a substitute** for the fix — a rotated password in a database that was readable is a convenience for the attacker, not a remedy — so it is the *second* action, after the version question, not the first.
5. **Is the 31 December 2026 WHMCS 9.0 end-of-life date going to affect my host?** — **Possibly, and that is exactly why it is worth asking.** The date is attested by the trade press covering the patch; the vendor's own end-of-life documentation could not be read when this article was written, so the article attributes it rather than presenting it as a vendor statement. The practical read for a buyer: a host on the 9.0 line has **35 days** left on that line, and the fix for all three flaws shipped *in* that line — so the host has to move twice (patch, then upgrade). That is a **host-viability and support-quality signal** to weigh before a yearly prepay, and it is a legitimate second question to put to the provider alongside the version question.
